# Qualitative Thesis Research: Circular Bio Economy Initiatives

This skill provides a systematic methodology for researching and analyzing **Circular Bio Economy** initiatives using qualitative research frameworks. Circular Bio Economy initiatives convert biological waste or byproducts — such as animal manure, agricultural residue, food waste, or organic material — into high-value goods (energy, compost, chemicals, materials, food, or bio-products) through biological processes (anaerobic digestion, fermentation, enzymatic hydrolysis, composting) or standard physicochemical treatment, with the goal of eliminating waste from biological value chains. The skill is designed for thesis research, policy analysis, or stakeholder mapping where you need to document 25+ such initiatives with structured metadata, institutional relationships, and public content availability.

## Research Goal

This skill supports a thesis investigating how fragmented Circular Bio Economy activities in Nepal can be understood, analyzed, and coordinated through Network orchestration mechanisms to support ecosystem emergence under conditions of limited state capacity. Comprehensive data collection on CBE initiatives is the foundation — the mapped data is then analyzed to identify barriers constraining CBE, coordination gaps between actors, and orchestration potential, leading to a context-sensitive orchestration model.

The **interactive dashboard** functions as an intermediary visualization tool: it makes the collected initiative data explorable and refinable, and helps surface patterns that feed the barrier and coordination gap analysis.

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill when you need to:

- Research 20+ Circular Bio Economy initiatives — organizations or programs that take biological waste or byproducts as inputs and produce high-value goods or energy as outputs
- Classify initiatives by institutional actor (Quintuple Helix: Government, Industry, Academia, Civil Society, Environment)
- Identify Circular Bio Economy pathway/sector (e.g., biogas, composting, waste valorization, biorefining, alternative bio-materials)
- Document how initiatives relate and coordinate within the Circular Bio Economy ecosystem
- Assess public content availability for qualitative analysis (academic papers, news articles, case studies, videos)
- Create an interactive research database for thesis committee, stakeholders, or policy makers

## Core Methodology

The skill uses a **Quintuple Helix framework** combined with **Circular Bio Economy sector classification** and **coordination mapping** to create a structured research database. This approach enables systematic comparison across Circular Bio Economy initiatives and identification of patterns in how biological waste streams are valorized across different institutional contexts.

### Quintuple Helix Classification

The Quintuple Helix identifies five actor categories involved in innovation ecosystems:

| Actor | Symbol | Definition |
|-------|--------|----------|
| **Government** | G | Ministries, regulatory agencies, public institutions, local government bodies, government-funded programs, extension services, and policy incentive schemes that set CBE-related policy, provide subsidies or financing, operate public CBE programs, or coordinate sector development. Includes: national ministries (Energy, Environment, Agriculture, Industry, Science), specialized agencies (AEPC, DoE, SWMTSC), local governments running waste management programs, government-sponsored training and extension services, and policy instruments (subsidies, tax incentives, feed-in tariffs, national programs). **Classification note:** A government agency primarily funding or enabling private sector CBE = G-I; a government body running a CBE program itself = G; a government-NGO partnership program = G-C. |
| **Industry** | I | Private companies, startups, social enterprises, commercial ventures, private financial institutions, private incubators, and private equity or venture capital funds that develop, produce, commercialize, or finance CBE activities. Includes: established CBE companies (composting, biogas, biorefining, bio-materials, bio-products), CBE startups and spin-offs, private R&D facilitators serving industry, industry associations, private commercial incubators (not university-affiliated), green/impact banks and microfinance institutions with CBE-specific products, and PE/VC funds actively investing in CBE ventures. **Classification note:** A private incubator → I; a university-affiliated incubator → A-I; a bank offering generic loans → not an actor; a bank with dedicated CBE green finance products → I or G-I if government co-funded. |
| **Academia** | A | Universities, government and private research centers, innovation hubs, innovation centers, and research labs that are **active participants** in CBE initiatives — conducting applied research, operating demonstration plants, providing technical training, or acting as named partners in government or industry CBE projects. Includes: university departments, national research councils (e.g., NARC, NAST, RECAST), private R&D institutes, technology incubators, and innovation hubs that translate CBE research into practice. Academia counts as an actor when the institution actively participates — not merely because papers about a topic exist. Outputs include: research findings, trained technicians, demonstration prototypes, incubated startups, policy recommendations, and knowledge brokering between actors. **Classification note:** Government-funded research centers = A-G; private for-profit R&D = A-I; independent non-profit research institutes = A; innovation hubs with mixed funding = A-I or A-G depending on primary funder. |
| **Civil Society** | C | Local NGOs, INGOs, community-based organizations (CBOs), women's groups and cooperatives, farmers' groups, biogas and waste user groups, youth organizations, faith-based organizations, and civic networks that mobilize communities, deliver CBE programs, advocate for policy change, or build capacity among CBE actors. Includes: local NGOs (WEPCO, BSP-Nepal, REC Nepal), INGOs (SNV, GIZ, Practical Action, HELVETAS, iDE, Clean Cooking Alliance, Winrock, Atmosfair), community biogas user groups, farmers' cooperatives with bio-waste activities, and women's cooperatives running bio-enterprises. **Classification note:** An INGO coordinating with government on program delivery = C-G; an INGO partnering with private sector CBE companies = C-I; an INGO with explicit environmental mandate = C-E. |
| **Environment** | E | Non-state organizations whose engagement with CBE is directly grounded in CBE principles: closing the biological loop, eliminating biological waste from linear disposal, regenerating ecosystems through biological cycling, or reducing GHG emissions from biological waste streams. **Qualifying test:** does this organization engage with CBE because biological waste is an environmental problem that CBE solves — or because CBE regenerates the ecosystem they protect? Includes: GHG/carbon credit bodies (Atmosfair, myclimate, Gold Standard, Verra/VCS, UNFCCC CDM projects), biological waste prevention organizations, ecosystem regeneration bodies that specifically value compost or digestate as a soil/ecosystem restoration tool, and environmental monitoring bodies for CBE installations. **Does NOT include:** organic certification bodies (certify product quality, not loop closure), generic conservation organizations without an explicit CBE-principle program, or broad eco-label schemes. **G vs E:** Government environmental agencies (DoE, MoFE) → G. **C vs E:** Community mobilization focus → C; CBE-principle environmental mission → E. |

Each initiative is classified by which actors are involved (e.g., "G-I" = Government-Industry partnership, "I-C-E" = Industry-Civil Society-Environment coalition, "A-I" = Academia-Industry research partnership).

### Sector Classification

Each initiative is classified by its primary Circular Bio Economy pathway — the specific route by which biological waste or byproduct is converted into a high-value output:

- **Biogas / Bio-energy** — Biological waste → energy via anaerobic digestion (biogas, bio-CNG, electricity)
- **Composting** — Organic waste → soil amendment via aerobic decomposition (compost, organic fertilizer), including vermicomposting and other composting utilizing agricultural residues and bio-waste.

- **Waste Valorization** — Biological waste → higher-value materials via physical/chemical conversion (fibers, bio-plastics, paper)
- **Biorefining** — Agricultural residues, agro-industrial byproducts, and biomass → high-value compounds via enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, or chemical fractionation (platform chemicals, 2G bioethanol, proteins, silica, furfural, biochar, phenolics). This is the primary CBE pathway for extracting maximum value from crop residues and agro-processing co-products.
- **Alternative Bio-Materials** — Renewable biological feedstock → substitutes for conventional materials (bio-leather, hemp textiles, plant-based packaging)
- **Bio-products** — Botanical or biological material → value-added end products (cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, biopesticides, supplements)
- **Waste-to-Feed / Waste-to-Food** — Biological waste, agricultural residues, and agro-industrial byproducts → protein, feed, food ingredients, or nutritional products via four sub-pathways: (a) **protein recycling** — recovering protein from slaughterhouse, fish, and dairy waste (blood meal, fishmeal, whey protein); (b) **protein upcycling** — converting low-value biological substrate into high-protein biomass via insect farming (BSF), Single Cell Protein (SCP) on agro-industrial waste, algae/spirulina on wastewater, mycoprotein on crop residues, or oilseed cake upgrading; (c) **nutritional loop closure** — recovering amino acids, minerals, vitamins, and bioactives from agro-industrial byproducts for food/feed fortification; (d) **food-grade ingredient extraction** — bioactives, dietary fiber, pectin, protein isolates, and natural colorants extracted from biological waste streams. **Note:** Traditional fermented food preparation (using fresh ingredients) is NOT a CBE activity — fermentation qualifies as CBE only when the substrate is a biological waste or residue.
- **Multi-pathway** — Integrated systems addressing multiple Circular Bio Economy pathways simultaneously

### Coordination and Barriers

Both fields use a **two-stage process** following Mayring's (2014) qualitative content analysis:

**Stage 1 — Data collection (in the JSON schema):** Extract observations verbatim or paraphrased from publicly available source content. Do not apply any category labels at this stage. Record what the source says, as specifically as possible.

**Stage 2 — Thematic coding (post-collection analysis):** Once observations are pooled across all initiatives, apply Mayring's inductive category development to derive categories from the material itself.

**`barriers`** — Record each constraint as described in the source (news articles, reports, interviews, government documents).

> Example: *"Biocomp Nepal struggled to secure commercial bank loans due to lack of collateral"* — not *"financial barrier"*

**`coordination`** — Record each coordination relationship or its explicit absence as described in the source.

> Example: *"BSP-Nepal receives technical backstopping from SNV Netherlands"* or *"No formal partnership with any academic institution identified"*

No pre-set category labels (e.g., financial / policy / capacity) are applied during data collection. These emerge from the thematic coding process in Phase 6.

### Exclusion Keywords

Filter out initiatives where source content primarily signals these non-CBE activities. CBE is strictly defined by **biological inputs** and **biological or physicochemical treatment** — activities that recycle or upcycle non-biological materials, or that manage waste without valorization, fall outside its scope regardless of how "circular" or "sustainable" they appear.

| Reason for exclusion | Exclusion keywords |
|----------------------|--------------------|
| **Synthetic textile recycling / upcycling** | polyester recycling, nylon recycling, synthetic fabric upcycling, fast fashion recycling, textile waste recycling, clothing recycling, garment upcycling, synthetic fiber recycling — **Note:** recycling of natural fiber textiles (cotton, wool, jute) may appear CBE-adjacent but does not qualify unless the input is explicitly a biological agricultural waste/residue and the process closes a biological loop |
| **Metal recycling / upcycling** | metal recycling, steel recycling, aluminium recycling, copper recycling, scrap metal, metal upcycling, iron recycling — inorganic materials entirely outside biological value chains |
| **Synthetic plastic recycling / upcycling** | PET recycling, polypropylene recycling, plastic bottle recycling, synthetic plastic upcycling, plastic-to-fuel, plastic waste recycling, plastic pellet recycling — **Note:** bio-plastic *production* from biological inputs (cornstarch, sugarcane bagasse) IS CBE; recycling of synthetic petroleum-based plastics is NOT |
| **Glass recycling** | glass recycling, cullet processing, glass bottle recycling — inorganic material |
| **E-waste / electronic recycling** | e-waste recycling, electronic waste, circuit board recycling, mobile phone recycling, battery recycling |
| **Rubber recycling** | tyre recycling, rubber recycling, vulcanized rubber processing |
| **Non-biological construction waste** | construction debris recycling, concrete recycling, demolition waste — unless specifically bio-based building materials from agricultural residues |
| **Fossil fuel energy** | coal, diesel, petroleum, natural gas, conventional electricity, fossil fuel |
| **Waste disposal without valorization** | landfill, incineration, waste dumping, waste transport only, sewage disposal |
| **Non-biological input without biological loop** | industrial chemical waste, heavy metals, synthetic dyes, petrochemical waste |
| **No value recovery** | waste management only, sanitation only, sewage treatment only, waste collection only |

> **Applying the exclusion test:** If the initiative's primary activity is recycling or upcycling of synthetic, inorganic, or non-biological materials — even if the company uses the words "circular," "sustainable," or "eco-friendly" — exclude from the CBE database. The defining question is always: **is the primary input a biological waste, agricultural residue, or agro-industrial byproduct?** If not, exclude.

## Research Workflow

### Phase 1: Research Planning

Define your research scope:

1. **Geography** — Which country or region? (e.g., "Circular Bio Economy in Nepal")
2. **Pathway scope** — All Circular Bio Economy pathways, or specific ones? (e.g., "Biogas and composting only")
3. **Initiative count target** — Aim for 25+ initiatives minimum
4. **Actor focus** — All Quintuple Helix actors, or specific focus? (e.g., "NGO/INGO/Government only")
5. **Time horizon** — Recent initiatives, or historical? (e.g., "Established 1990–2026")
6. **Content requirement** — Minimum public content level acceptable? (e.g., "Medium-High or higher")

### Phase 6: Thematic Coding (Mayring, 2014)

Once the initiative database is complete and loaded into the dashboard, apply Mayring's inductive category development to the pooled `barriers` and `coordination` observations across all initiatives. This phase transforms raw observations into a scientific coding framework.

**Step 1 — Define the coding unit**
The coding unit is a single observation string within a `barriers` or `coordination` array. Each string is treated as one unit of meaning.

**Step 2 — Open coding**
Read through all observations without applying pre-defined categories. Assign a short descriptive label to each observation that captures its essential meaning.

> *"Struggled to secure bank loans"* → label: `credit access barrier`
> *"Receives technical training from BSP-Nepal"* → label: `technical support relationship`

**Step 3 — Inductive category formation**
Group similar open codes into emerging categories. Categories are defined by what is found in the material — not imposed in advance. After processing approximately 30–50% of observations, pause and revise the emerging category scheme for consistency.

**Step 4 — Category revision and definition**
For each category, write:
- A clear **definition** (what belongs in this category)
- An **anchor example** (a prototypical observation from the data)
- **Coding rules** (how to handle borderline cases)

**Step 5 — Final coding pass**
Apply the finalized category scheme to all observations across all initiatives. Each observation receives one primary category label (and optionally a secondary label if it spans two categories). Store coded results in `barriers_coded` and `coordination_coded` — **the original `barriers` and `coordination` arrays are never modified**. The originals remain as the audit trail and source of truth.

**Step 6 — Inter-coder reliability check**
Have a second coder independently apply the category scheme to a random 20% sample of observations. Calculate agreement using Cohen's Kappa (target: κ ≥ 0.70). Resolve disagreements through discussion and refine coding rules as needed.

**Step 7 — Interpretation**
Aggregate coded observations to identify:
- For `barriers`: which constraint types dominate, which sectors or actor types face them most
- For `coordination`: which relationship types are present, which are absent, and where coordination gaps cluster

This coded output feeds directly into the barrier analysis, coordination gap analysis, and orchestration potential evaluation stages of the thesis.

## Best Practices

**Search comprehensively:** Use multiple search methods and languages. For international research, search in local languages (e.g., Nepali for Nepal projects).

**Verify recursively:** When you find one initiative, search for similar ones it mentions or partners with. This reveals ecosystem clusters and hidden initiatives.

**Document sources rigorously:** Maintain complete URLs and source citations for every data point. This enables thesis readers to verify findings independently.

**Assess content quality:** Not all initiatives have equal documentation. Prioritize those with "High" or "Very High" public content levels for qualitative analysis.

**Update continuously:** Research is iterative. As you discover new initiatives, update your database and re-run visualizations to reveal emerging patterns.

**Engage stakeholders:** Share preliminary findings with sector experts, NGOs, or government bodies. They often know of initiatives not yet in public domain.

## Example: Nepal Circular Bio Economy Research

This skill was developed through research on Nepal's Circular Bio Economy sector, resulting in a database of 24 initiatives spanning private companies (Biocomp Nepal, HBR Nepal), local NGOs (WEPCO, BSP-Nepal), government bodies (AEPC), and international organizations (Atmosfair, UNDP, WPD). The research identified a formal, coordinated ecosystem around household biogas (240,000+ plants installed) and emerging opportunities in biorefining and bio-materials.
