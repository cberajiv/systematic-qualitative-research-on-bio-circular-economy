# Sector Taxonomy: Circular Bio Economy

This document defines the eight Circular Bio Economy pathway categories used to classify initiatives. A Circular Bio Economy initiative must have a **biological waste, agricultural residue, or agro-industrial byproduct as its primary input** and produce a **high-value output** (energy, material, chemical, food, or bio-product) with minimal residual waste. Each pathway below represents a distinct route for achieving this.

**Two distinct input categories are covered:**
- **Biological waste** — material discarded from biological processes (animal manure, food waste, organic municipal waste, slaughterhouse waste)
- **Agricultural residues and agro-industrial byproducts** — co-products of crop production and processing that retain significant resource value (rice straw, sugarcane bagasse, oilseed cake, molasses, spice processing waste, fruit pomace). These are not "waste" in the conventional sense — they are underutilized biological resources whose valorization into high-value products is the defining CBE opportunity.

## Primary Sectors

### 1. Biogas / Bio-Energy

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert organic waste or biomass into biogas (methane) through anaerobic digestion, or produce other forms of renewable energy from biological materials.

**Key processes:**
- Anaerobic digestion of animal manure, agricultural residue, or food waste
- Biogas production for cooking fuel or electricity generation
- Bio-CNG production (compressed biogas for vehicles)
- Biogas plant construction and maintenance

**Typical actors:** Government (AEPC), NGOs (BSP-Nepal), Private companies (biogas manufacturers), Communities (household digesters)

**Impact metrics:** CO₂ reduction, biogas volume produced, households/facilities served, jobs created

**Examples:**
- Alternative Energy Promotion Centre (AEPC) — Policy and subsidy coordination
- Biogas Sector Partnership - Nepal (BSP-Nepal) — Implementation and training
- Gandaki Urja — Commercial biogas production
- Household biogas digesters — Community-level energy production

**Public content level:** Usually Very High to High (well-documented sector with government support)

---

### 2. Composting

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert organic waste (food scraps, agricultural residue, market waste) into compost or organic fertilizer through aerobic decomposition.

**Key processes:**
- Aerobic decomposition of organic waste
- Compost production for agricultural use
- Organic fertilizer production
- Waste collection and processing

**Typical actors:** Private social enterprises, NGOs, Communities, Government (waste management)

**Impact metrics:** Waste volume processed, compost produced, CO₂ avoided, jobs created, farmers served

**Examples:**
- Biocomp Nepal — Large-scale commercial composting
- Community composting centers — Grassroots waste management
- Vermicomposting initiatives — Worm-based composting

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium-High (growing documentation)

---

### 3. Waste Valorization

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert **biological waste or agricultural residues** into higher-value materials, chemicals, or compounds through physical, chemical, or biological processes. The input must be a biological material — synthetic, inorganic, or petroleum-based materials fall outside this sector regardless of how the conversion is described.

**Key processes:**
- Waste-to-fiber conversion (e.g., banana trunk, hemp stem, jute stalk to natural fiber)
- Bio-plastic production (e.g., corn starch, sugarcane bagasse, potato starch → bio-based plastic — note: this is *production* from biological input, not recycling of synthetic plastic)
- Waste-to-paper conversion (e.g., elephant dung, agricultural residue → paper)
- Agricultural residue-to-material conversion (e.g., rice husk board, bagasse packaging, bamboo composite)

**Exclusion boundary:**
- Synthetic plastic recycling → **excluded** (petroleum-based polymer, not biological input)
- Textile recycling of synthetic fibers → **excluded** (non-biological input)
- Metal or glass recycling → **excluded** (inorganic materials)
- Mechanical recycling of non-biological materials → **excluded** regardless of "circular" or "eco" labelling

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Social enterprises, Startups, NGOs

**Impact metrics:** Biological waste volume processed, product value, jobs created, market adoption

**Examples:**
- Ecokanduk — Banana trunk and hemp stem to natural fiber (biological agricultural waste → fiber)
- Zero Circular — Corn starch to bio-plastic (biological agricultural byproduct → bio-based material)
- Ecoorb Ventures — Elephant dung to paper and bags (biological waste → paper)
- Agricultural residue building material companies — rice husk, bagasse, bamboo → construction materials

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (emerging sector with limited documentation)

---

### 4. Biorefining

**Definition:** Initiatives that extract high-value compounds from **agricultural residues, agro-industrial byproducts, or biomass** using enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, chemical fractionation, or thermochemical processes. This is the primary CBE pathway for maximizing value recovery from crop residues and processing co-products that would otherwise be burned, ploughed under, or discarded.

**Primary inputs (agricultural residue / agro-industrial byproduct focus):**
- Lignocellulosic crop residues: rice straw, wheat straw, maize stover, sugarcane bagasse, jute stalks, bamboo, hemp stalks
- Agro-industrial byproducts: rice husk, molasses, oilseed cake (mustard, sunflower, soybean), distillery spent wash, brewery spent grain, dairy whey
- Spice and herbal processing waste: cardamom husks, ginger fiber, turmeric residue, tea leaf waste
- Fruit and vegetable processing waste: fruit pomace, peels, pulp residues

**Key processes:**
- Enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose/hemicellulose in crop residues → fermentable sugars → 2G bioethanol
- Fermentation of agro-industrial sugars → platform chemicals (lactic acid, succinic acid, xylitol)
- Chemical fractionation of rice husk → amorphous silica
- Hydrothermal or pyrolytic conversion of agricultural residues → biochar
- Extraction of phenolic compounds, antioxidants, and bioactives from spice/herbal processing waste
- Protein concentration from oilseed cake and agricultural residues

**Typical actors:** Private biorefining companies, University research groups, Government research institutes (NARC, NAST), Agro-processing industries generating the residues

**Impact metrics:** Feedstock utilization rate, compound yield per tonne of residue, product value per unit input, jobs created, agricultural residue diverted from open burning

**Nepal-specific examples:**
- Himalayan Bio Refineries (HBR) Nepal — 2G bioethanol from bamboo and rice straw
- Nawa Paila / SWAT Labs — Bio-hythane and platform chemicals from agro-residues
- University applied research programs — Enzymatic hydrolysis of Nepal's lignocellulosic residues

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (research-heavy sector; commercial documentation limited but growing)

---

### 5. Alternative Bio-Materials

**Definition:** Initiatives that **produce** bio-based materials from biological waste, agricultural residues, or renewable biological feedstock as substitutes for conventional synthetic or animal-derived materials. The defining feature is *production* from a biological input — not recycling or upcycling of existing synthetic or conventional materials.

**Key processes:**
- Mycelium-based leather production (fungal biomass grown on agricultural residues → leather substitute)
- Plant-based leather from agricultural waste byproducts (banana stem, pineapple leaf fiber, cactus)
- Bio-plastic production from biological feedstock (cornstarch, sugarcane bagasse, cellulose → bio-based plastic)
- Natural fiber textiles from agricultural residues (hemp stalk, jute, banana fiber → textile fiber)
- Bio-based packaging from agricultural residues (bagasse, rice husk, bamboo → packaging material)

**Exclusion boundary:**
- Recycling or upcycling of synthetic textiles → **excluded** (non-biological input)
- Recycling of conventional plastic, metal, or glass → **excluded** (not biological feedstock)
- Fashion or textile upcycling of existing garments → **excluded** unless input is biological agricultural waste
- "Eco-fashion" or "sustainable fashion" using synthetic recycled materials → **excluded**

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Startups, Social enterprises, Research institutes

**Impact metrics:** Material volume produced, biological waste/residue utilized, product adoption, synthetic material substituted, environmental benefits, jobs created

**Examples:**
- Royal Hemp Nepal — Hemp stalk (agricultural residue) → fiber for footwear and apparel
- Mycelium leather initiatives — Fungal biomass on agricultural residues → bio-leather
- Zero Circular — Cornstarch (agro-byproduct) → bio-plastic
- Plant-based leather startups — Banana stem, pineapple leaf fiber (agricultural waste) → alternative leather

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium (growing media interest in sustainable materials)

---

### 6. Bio-Products

**Definition:** Initiatives that produce value-added products (cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food supplements, pesticides) from botanical or biological materials.

**Key processes:**
- Botanical extraction for cosmetics and skincare
- Medicinal plant processing for pharmaceuticals
- Protein extraction for food supplements
- Biopesticide production from plant materials
- Herbal product formulation

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Social enterprises, Traditional knowledge practitioners, Research institutes

**Impact metrics:** Product revenue, market adoption, jobs created, biodiversity impact

**Examples:**
- Herveda Botanicals — Ethical skincare from medicinal herbs
- Biopesticide companies — Plant-based pest management
- Herbal supplement producers — Value-added botanical products
- Cosmetics companies using local botanicals — Bio-based beauty products

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium (commercial sector with good media coverage)

### 7. Waste-to-Feed / Waste-to-Food: Protein Recycling, Upcycling, and Nutritional Valorization

**Definition:** Initiatives that recover, recycle, or upcycle protein and other nutrients from biological waste streams, agricultural residues, and agro-industrial byproducts into animal feed, human food products, functional food ingredients, or nutritional supplements — closing the protein and nutrient loop within biological value chains and contributing to food security.

**Four scientifically distinct sub-pathways:**

#### 7a. Protein Recycling
Recovering protein that already exists in protein-rich biological waste streams and returning it to food or feed chains with minimal transformation.

- **Inputs:** Slaughterhouse waste (blood, offal, bone, feathers), fish processing waste, dairy whey, egg processing waste
- **Outputs:** Blood meal, bone meal, meat meal, feather meal, fishmeal, whey protein concentrate
- **Processes:** Rendering, hydrolysis, drying, fractionation
- **Food security relevance:** Recovers high-quality protein already present in waste; prevents nutritional value from being lost to landfill or incineration

#### 7b. Protein Upcycling
Converting low-protein or low-value biological materials into high-protein biomass through biological processes — increasing protein density and nutritional value beyond the original substrate.

- **Insect protein (BSF and other species):**
  - Black Soldier Fly (BSF) larvae convert organic waste (food waste, manure, agricultural residues) into larval biomass containing 40–60% protein and 30% fat
  - Frass (insect excrement) is a high-value organic fertilizer co-product
  - Outputs: insect meal for aquaculture and poultry feed; emerging human food applications

- **Single Cell Protein (SCP):**
  - Bacteria, yeast (Saccharomyces, Candida), or fungi grown on agricultural residues, molasses, distillery spent wash, or agro-industrial effluents
  - Biomass harvested and dried → 40–80% crude protein depending on organism
  - Nepal relevance: agricultural residue abundance (rice straw, sugarcane molasses) as SCP substrate

- **Algal and aquatic protein:**
  - Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis) cultivated on wastewater or CO₂-rich streams → 60–70% protein, complete amino acid profile, micronutrients; dual function: waste treatment + high-value food
  - Chlorella and microalgae cultivated on agricultural effluents → protein and omega-3 enrichment
  - Duckweed (Lemna spp.) cultivated on wastewater or digestate → 25–45% protein; fast-growing aquatic biomass for feed and food

- **Mycoprotein / Fungal protein:**
  - Fungi (Fusarium, Aspergillus, Trichoderma) grown on lignocellulosic agricultural residues → protein-rich fungal biomass
  - Solid-state fermentation of crop residues → mycoprotein for animal feed or human food ingredient

- **Oilseed cake protein upcycling:**
  - Mustard cake, rapeseed meal, soybean meal — byproducts of oil extraction with 30–40% crude protein
  - Processes: enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, heat treatment to reduce antinutrients → upgraded feed or food-grade protein
  - Nepal relevance: significant mustard oil production generates abundant mustard cake

#### 7c. Nutritional Loop Closure
Recovering micro- and macro-nutrients (amino acids, minerals, vitamins) from food waste and biological residues and returning them to food systems — closing the nutritional loop to address deficiency-related food insecurity.

- **Amino acid extraction:** Hydrolysis of protein-rich bio-waste → individual amino acids for food fortification or feed supplementation
- **Mineral recovery:** Recovering phosphorus, calcium, iron, and zinc from biological waste for food/feed fortification
- **Vitamin production:** Fermentation of agricultural residues to produce B vitamins (B2, B12), vitamin K
- **Biofortification:** Enzymatic or biological treatment of agro-industrial byproducts (e.g., phytase treatment of oilseed meal to improve mineral bioavailability) — valid only when the substrate is a biological waste or byproduct, not fresh produce
- **Functional food ingredients:** Dietary fiber, prebiotics, and bioactive compounds extracted from agro-industrial byproducts (fruit pomace → pectin, spent grain → dietary fiber, rice bran → gamma-oryzanol) for food enrichment

> **Important scientific boundary:** Fermentation is a process, not a CBE indicator. Fermentation qualifies as CBE only when the **substrate is a biological waste, residue, or byproduct** — not when fresh agricultural produce is the input. Traditional fermented foods (gundruk, kinema, sinki, chaang, rakshi) use fresh vegetables or grains as inputs — they are food preservation practices, not CBE activities, and must not be included.

#### 7d. Food-Grade Ingredient Extraction from Biological Waste
Initiatives that extract food-grade or feed-grade ingredients directly from biological waste streams and agro-industrial byproducts — the highest-value outcome in the nutritional CBE loop.

- **Bioactive compound extraction:** Polyphenols, antioxidants, carotenoids, and flavonoids from fruit and vegetable processing waste (pomace, peels, seeds) → functional food ingredients
- **Dietary fiber and prebiotic extraction:** From spent grain, fruit pomace, bran, oilseed hulls → fiber-enriched food ingredients
- **Food-grade protein isolates:** From oilseed processing byproducts (mustard cake, soybean meal) → upgraded food-grade protein using enzymatic or physical fractionation
- **Pectin and hydrocolloids:** From fruit pomace (apple, citrus) → natural food thickeners and stabilizers
- **Natural colorants and flavors:** From plant processing waste → food-grade colorants extracted from biological waste streams

**Food security framing:**
In contexts of food and nutrition insecurity (Nepal: 23% child wasting, significant protein-energy malnutrition), CBE pathways that close protein and nutrient loops are not merely environmental initiatives — they are food system resilience mechanisms. Alternative proteins from biological waste (insect, algal, SCP) reduce dependence on land- and water-intensive conventional protein production.

**Typical actors:** Insect farming startups, alternative protein companies, nutrition research institutes (Agriculture and Forestry University, Tribhuvan University food science), food security INGOs (WFP, FAO, Action Against Hunger), government nutrition programs (National Nutrition Programme, MoALD food security division)

**Impact metrics:** Protein yield per tonne of input waste, amino acid profile completeness, waste volume diverted, households reached with affordable protein, contribution to dietary diversity, CO₂ reduction from avoided waste disposal

**Nepal-specific examples:**
- CiBiS (Circular Bioeconomy Solutions) — BSF insect farming on organic waste → protein meal and frass fertilizer
- Spirulina cultivation ventures — algal protein from wastewater or CO₂ waste streams
- Mustard cake protein valorization — enzymatic upgrading of oilseed processing byproduct to food/feed-grade protein
- Rice bran bioactive extraction — gamma-oryzanol and dietary fiber from rice milling byproduct
- Fruit pomace ingredient extraction — bioactive compounds and pectin from fruit processing waste

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (rapidly growing sector; insect farming and algal protein increasingly documented; SCP and nutritional loop closure have limited Nepal-specific documentation)

### 8. Multi-Pathway

**Definition:** Initiatives that integrate multiple Circular Bio Economy pathways (biogas, composting, biorefining, waste valorization) into comprehensive systems.

**Key processes:**
- Integrated waste management systems (collection → sorting → multiple pathways)
- Biorefinery complexes (multiple product streams from single feedstock)
- Circular economy hubs (multiple initiatives in coordinated ecosystem)
- Material recovery facilities with multiple processing lines

**Typical actors:** Government programs, Large NGOs, INGOs, Coordinated private-public partnerships

**Impact metrics:** Multiple metrics across pathways, integrated waste management efficiency, ecosystem coordination

**Examples:**
- UNDP Nepal Circular & Green Economy Programme — Multiple pathways (biogas, composting, biochar)
- Roots of Circularity Programme — Integrated circular economy approach
- Coordinated biogas + composting + biorefining systems

**Public content level:** Usually Very High to High (well-documented multi-stakeholder programs)

## Sector Selection Criteria

When classifying an initiative, use these criteria:

1. **Primary activity:** What is the main output or service? (e.g., biogas production → Biogas/Bio-Energy)
2. **Process type:** What technology or process is used? (e.g., enzymatic hydrolysis → Biorefining)
3. **Product value:** Is the output energy, material, or high-value compound? (e.g., high-value compound → Biorefining)
4. **Scale:** Is it household-level, commercial, or research-level?
5. **Integration:** Does it combine multiple pathways? (e.g., biogas + composting → Multi-pathway)

## Classification Boundary

When assessing whether an initiative belongs in the Circular Bio Economy database, apply this three-question test:

1. **Is the primary input a biological waste, agricultural residue, or agro-industrial byproduct?** (animal manure, agricultural residue, food waste, organic material, biomass, agro-processing co-product) — if no, **exclude**
2. **Is the primary output a high-value good, energy, useful material, or nutritional product?** — if the output is simply waste disposal with no value recovery, **exclude**
3. **Is a biological or physicochemical treatment process used to achieve the conversion?** — if yes, **include**

**Explicitly excluded activity types — regardless of "circular," "sustainable," or "eco" labelling:**

| Activity | Reason for exclusion |
|----------|---------------------|
| Synthetic textile recycling / upcycling (polyester, nylon, synthetic blends) | Non-biological input; petroleum-based polymer |
| Metal recycling / upcycling (steel, aluminium, copper, scrap) | Inorganic material; outside biological value chains |
| Synthetic plastic recycling (PET, PP, HDPE, mixed plastics) | Petroleum-based polymer; not a biological waste stream — **note:** bio-plastic *production* from cornstarch or sugarcane is CBE; recycling of synthetic plastic is not |
| Glass recycling | Inorganic material |
| E-waste / electronic waste recycling | Inorganic and synthetic materials |
| Rubber recycling (synthetic) | Synthetic polymer |
| Fashion upcycling / textile upcycling (non-biological) | Repurposing of synthetic or mixed-fiber garments is not biological loop closure |
| Mechanical recycling of any non-biological material | No biological input, no biological process |
| Waste disposal without valorization | Landfill, incineration, transport-only operations |
| Fossil fuel energy production | Coal, diesel, petroleum, natural gas |

**Ambiguous cases requiring investigation:**
- **Natural fiber textile recycling** (cotton, wool, jute, hemp): borderline — recycling of used natural fiber textiles is not CBE unless the input is specifically a biological agricultural waste/residue and the process explicitly closes a biological loop; if unclear, exclude
- **Bio-plastic recycling** (recycling of PLA or other bio-based plastics): the original production from biological inputs is CBE; downstream mechanical recycling of the bio-plastic is not in itself CBE unless the recycling process re-enters a biological value chain
- **Paper recycling**: recycling of paper not sourced from biological waste/residues is excluded; production of paper FROM biological waste (elephant dung, agricultural residue) is included
