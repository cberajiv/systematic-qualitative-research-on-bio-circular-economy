# Sector Taxonomy: Circular Bio Economy

This document defines the eight Circular Bio Economy pathway categories used to classify initiatives. A Circular Bio Economy initiative must have a **biological waste or byproduct as its primary input** and produce a **high-value output** (energy, material, chemical, food, or bio-product) with minimal residual waste. Each pathway below represents a distinct route for achieving this.

## Primary Sectors

### 1. Biogas / Bio-Energy

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert organic waste or biomass into biogas (methane) through anaerobic digestion, or produce other forms of renewable energy from biological materials.

**Key processes:**
- Anaerobic digestion of animal manure, agricultural residue, or food waste
- Biogas production for cooking fuel or electricity generation
- Bio-CNG production (compressed biogas for vehicles)
- Biogas plant construction and maintenance

**Typical actors:** Government (AEPC), NGOs (BSP-Nepal), Private companies (biogas manufacturers), Communities (household digesters)

**Impact metrics:** CO₂ reduction, biogas volume produced, households/facilities served, jobs created

**Examples:**
- Alternative Energy Promotion Centre (AEPC) — Policy and subsidy coordination
- Biogas Sector Partnership - Nepal (BSP-Nepal) — Implementation and training
- Gandaki Urja — Commercial biogas production
- Household biogas digesters — Community-level energy production

**Public content level:** Usually Very High to High (well-documented sector with government support)

---

### 2. Composting

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert organic waste (food scraps, agricultural residue, market waste) into compost or organic fertilizer through aerobic decomposition.

**Key processes:**
- Aerobic decomposition of organic waste
- Compost production for agricultural use
- Organic fertilizer production
- Waste collection and processing

**Typical actors:** Private social enterprises, NGOs, Communities, Government (waste management)

**Impact metrics:** Waste volume processed, compost produced, CO₂ avoided, jobs created, farmers served

**Examples:**
- Biocomp Nepal — Large-scale commercial composting
- Community composting centers — Grassroots waste management
- Vermicomposting initiatives — Worm-based composting

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium-High (growing documentation)

---

### 3. Waste Valorization

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert waste materials into higher-value products (chemicals, materials, compounds) through physical, chemical, or biological processes.

**Key processes:**
- Waste-to-fiber conversion (e.g., banana trunk to fiber)
- Waste-to-plastic conversion (e.g., corn starch to bio-plastic)
- Waste-to-paper conversion (e.g., elephant dung to paper)
- Waste-to-material conversion (e.g., agricultural residue to building materials)

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Social enterprises, Startups, NGOs

**Impact metrics:** Waste volume processed, product value, jobs created, market adoption

**Examples:**
- Ecokanduk — Banana trunk and hemp stem to fiber
- Zero Circular — Corn starch to bio-plastic
- Ecoorb Ventures — Elephant dung to paper and bags
- Creasion Nepal — Plastic waste recycling (non-bio)

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (emerging sector with limited documentation)

---

### 4. Biorefining

**Definition:** Initiatives that use advanced enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, or chemical processes to extract high-value compounds (platform chemicals, enzymes, proteins) from biomass or bio-waste.

**Key processes:**
- Enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose/hemicellulose
- Fermentation for second-generation bioethanol
- Platform chemical extraction (e.g., lactic acid, succinic acid)
- Protein extraction from agricultural waste
- Bioactive compound extraction (e.g., polyphenols, antioxidants)

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Research institutes, Startups, Government research programs

**Impact metrics:** Compound yield, product value, feedstock efficiency, jobs created, market adoption

**Examples:**
- Himalayan Bio Refineries (HBR) Nepal — 2G bioethanol from bamboo and rice straw
- Nawa Paila / SWAT Labs — Bio-hythane and platform chemicals research
- Enzymatic hydrolysis pilots — Research initiatives

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (research-heavy sector, limited commercial documentation)

---

### 5. Alternative Materials

**Definition:** Initiatives that produce bio-based materials (leather, textiles, packaging) from renewable resources as substitutes for conventional materials.

**Key processes:**
- Mycelium-based leather production
- Plant-based leather (e.g., cactus, pineapple, banana)
- Bio-plastic production from renewable feedstock
- Hemp or plant fiber textiles
- Bio-based packaging materials

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Startups, Social enterprises, Research institutes

**Impact metrics:** Material volume produced, product adoption, environmental benefits, jobs created

**Examples:**
- Royal Hemp Nepal — Hemp fiber footwear and apparel
- Mycelium leather initiatives — Bio-leather research
- Bio-plastic companies — Renewable plastic production
- Plant-based leather startups — Alternative material innovation

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium (growing media interest in sustainable materials)

---

### 6. Bio-Products

**Definition:** Initiatives that produce value-added products (cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food supplements, pesticides) from botanical or biological materials.

**Key processes:**
- Botanical extraction for cosmetics and skincare
- Medicinal plant processing for pharmaceuticals
- Protein extraction for food supplements
- Biopesticide production from plant materials
- Herbal product formulation

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Social enterprises, Traditional knowledge practitioners, Research institutes

**Impact metrics:** Product revenue, market adoption, jobs created, biodiversity impact

**Examples:**
- Herveda Botanicals — Ethical skincare from medicinal herbs
- Biopesticide companies — Plant-based pest management
- Herbal supplement producers — Value-added botanical products
- Cosmetics companies using local botanicals — Bio-based beauty products

**Public content level:** Usually High to Medium (commercial sector with good media coverage)

---

### 7. Waste-to-Feed / Waste-to-Food

**Definition:** Initiatives that convert agricultural or food waste into animal feed, human food products, or nutritional supplements.

**Key processes:**
- Insect farming on organic waste (e.g., black soldier fly larvae)
- Algae/spirulina cultivation on waste streams
- Protein extraction for animal feed
- Food waste processing into nutritional products
- Fermented food production from agricultural waste

**Typical actors:** Private companies, Social enterprises, Research institutes, Startups

**Impact metrics:** Feed/food volume produced, nutritional value, waste diverted, jobs created, market adoption

**Examples:**
- Circular Bioeconomy Solutions (CiBiS) — Insect-based waste recycling
- Algae cultivation on wastewater — Dual waste treatment and food production
- Fermentation-based food products — Value-added food from waste

**Public content level:** Usually Medium-High to Medium (emerging sector with growing interest)

---

### 8. Multi-Pathway

**Definition:** Initiatives that integrate multiple Circular Bio Economy pathways (biogas, composting, biorefining, waste valorization) into comprehensive systems.

**Key processes:**
- Integrated waste management systems (collection → sorting → multiple pathways)
- Biorefinery complexes (multiple product streams from single feedstock)
- Circular economy hubs (multiple initiatives in coordinated ecosystem)
- Material recovery facilities with multiple processing lines

**Typical actors:** Government programs, Large NGOs, INGOs, Coordinated private-public partnerships

**Impact metrics:** Multiple metrics across pathways, integrated waste management efficiency, ecosystem coordination

**Examples:**
- UNDP Nepal Circular & Green Economy Programme — Multiple pathways (biogas, composting, biochar)
- Roots of Circularity Programme — Integrated circular economy approach
- Coordinated biogas + composting + biorefining systems

**Public content level:** Usually Very High to High (well-documented multi-stakeholder programs)

---

## Sector Selection Criteria

When classifying an initiative, use these criteria:

1. **Primary activity:** What is the main output or service? (e.g., biogas production → Biogas/Bio-Energy)
2. **Process type:** What technology or process is used? (e.g., enzymatic hydrolysis → Biorefining)
3. **Product value:** Is the output energy, material, or high-value compound? (e.g., high-value compound → Biorefining)
4. **Scale:** Is it household-level, commercial, or research-level?
5. **Integration:** Does it combine multiple pathways? (e.g., biogas + composting → Multi-pathway)

## Sector Distribution Analysis

For your research database, calculate:

- **Sector count:** How many initiatives per sector?
- **Sector maturity:** Which sectors are mature (many initiatives) vs. emerging (few initiatives)?
- **Sector gaps:** Which sectors have few initiatives (research opportunities)?
- **Sector coordination:** Which sectors have formal coordination (ecosystem maturity)?

## Classification Boundary

When assessing whether an initiative belongs in the Circular Bio Economy database, apply this test:

1. **Is the primary input a biological waste or byproduct?** (animal manure, agricultural residue, food waste, organic material, biomass) — if no, exclude
2. **Is the primary output a high-value good, energy, or useful material?** — if the output is simply waste disposal with no value recovery, exclude
3. **Is a biological or physicochemical treatment process used to achieve the conversion?** — if yes, include

Initiatives that are purely mechanical recycling (non-biological), conventional fossil-fuel energy, or waste disposal without valorization fall outside the Circular Bio Economy scope and should not be included in the research database.
